Today's idea

Can Everything Be Priced?

Until now, all the thinkers in our chapter spoke about one thing: how a price is determined. Smith argued it's labor. Marx - exploitation. Jevons - marginal utility. Marshall - the meeting point.

All of them, in some way, believed that economics is neutral - that it has internal rules that work independently of values.

In 2012, an American philosopher named Michael Sandel, from Harvard University, published a book that challenged all of this. "What Money Can't Buy."

His claim is simple and hard: not everything should be in the market. And there are things that, when brought into the market - become something else.

A kidney. Friendship. A vote. Poetry. We can give them a price. The question is - should we?

Think it through

A quick check

A hospital offers: "Donate a kidney - receive 50,000 shekels." The number of donors drops significantly. Why, according to Sandel?

This is the heart of Sandel. The market is not neutral - it changes the meaning of the thing it touches. A donation without compensation expresses values of generosity and solidarity. When it becomes a 50,000-shekel transaction, it loses its moral character. Donors who would do it out of values - stop, because "it's no longer the same thing."
